# What-If Generation

A spark is not yet a story because nothing in it is under pressure. A
what-if puts it under pressure: someone wants something, and the spark
makes that hard. Generate widely, then let the user choose.

## Start from the kind of spark

| Spark | Ask first | Pressure usually comes from |
|-------|-----------|-----------------------------|
| Image (a drowned bell tower) | Who is looking at it, and why does it matter to them? | The history the image implies, or what happens if it changes |
| What-if (grief can be sold) | Who gains, who loses, who is the first to refuse? | The rule's side effects and the people it hurts |
| Character (a keeper who never leaves the rock) | What would force them to do the thing they never do? | The one situation their nature cannot handle |
| Setting (a city that moves every winter) | Who is left behind, and who decides the route? | The setting's rules and the people who enforce them |
| Feeling (dread in a happy house) | What event would earn that feeling honestly? | The gap between how things look and what is true |

## Angles

Run the spark through several angles. Each line of output is one short
sentence starting "What if".

1. **Invert it.** Flip who holds power, who is guilty, what is normal.
   *What if the ghost is the one being haunted?*
2. **Raise the cost.** Make the spark expensive for the person closest to
   it. *What if selling grief also sells the memory of the person?*
3. **Give it to the wrong person.** The least equipped, the most
   compromised, the one who benefits from it staying broken.
4. **Put a clock on it.** A deadline, a season, a tide, a vote, a
   pregnancy, a sentence.
5. **Move it.** Another century, another class, another country, a
   smaller scale (one street) or a larger one (a whole nation).
6. **Make it personal.** Tie the spark to a relationship: the sibling, the
   ex, the parent, the child.
7. **Find the first.** The first person to discover, refuse, exploit, or
   be harmed by the spark.
8. **Find the last.** The last keeper, the last believer, the last one who
   remembers.
9. **Ask who profits.** Every strange rule has someone who benefits from
   it. That person is often the antagonist.
10. **Collide genres.** Put the spark in a heist, a romance, a
    courtroom, a quest. Genre supplies a shape and reader expectations
    (see the `genre-craft` skill).

## Worked example

Spark: *a lighthouse keeper who has never left the rock.*

1. What if the lighthouse is decommissioned and she has thirty days to leave?
2. What if a boat wrecks on the rock and the only survivor claims to be her sister?
3. What if she has been told the mainland was destroyed, and it was not?
4. What if her keeping is the only thing stopping something at sea from coming ashore?
5. What if the keeper is twelve, and her father has not come back from the supply run?

Lines 1, 2, and 5 already imply a want, an obstacle, and a clock. Line 3
is a twist, not a premise: ask what the story is before the reveal. Line
4 is a genre shift toward horror or fantasy; offer it, do not push it.

## Filtering

Before presenting, drop what-ifs that:

- Have no person in them (a pure world concept with nobody to suffer it)
- Resolve in one scene (the answer is obvious once asked)
- Depend entirely on a twist, with nothing to dramatise before it
- Repeat another line with different nouns

Keep 8-12. Mark the ones that already imply conflict; let the user pick.
Combining two what-ifs is often the strongest move: a character spark plus
a setting spark gives both a person and pressure.
