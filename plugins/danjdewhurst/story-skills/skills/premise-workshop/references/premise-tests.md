# Premise Tests

Run these against each logline (recipe in
`../../story-init/references/title-logline.md`). Report each as **pass**,
**weak**, or **fail** with one sentence of why, and offer one revision for
each weak or failed test. A premise can fail a test on purpose (a quiet
literary novel may have a passive protagonist by design); record the
choice and move on.

## Logline stress tests

| Test | Question | Common fix |
|------|----------|-----------|
| Active protagonist | Does the protagonist pursue something, or does the story happen to them? | Give them a goal they choose, even a wrong one |
| Concrete want | Could you film the moment they get it or lose it? | Replace an abstract want (*to be free*) with its visible form (*to get the boat off the rock before the storm*) |
| Opposition that can win | Is the obstacle a person, system, or force strong enough that failure is plausible? | Make the antagonist want the same thing, or give them a legitimate claim |
| Built-in conflict | Does the premise force a collision, or only allow one? | Tie the want to the obstacle: getting it must cost the protagonist something they value |
| A choice at the end | Can you imagine the final choice the protagonist faces? | Name two things they value and design the ending to make them pick |
| Irony or hook | Is there something surprising: the wrong person, an unexpected cost, a contradiction? | Apply "give it to the wrong person" from `what-if-generation.md` |
| Sustains the length | Does the situation have enough turns for the chosen form? | See `form-choice.md`; shrink the form or add a complicating thread |
| Distinct | Could this logline describe a well-known book with names changed? | Change the protagonist, the cost, or the setting until it could not |

## Stakes on three levels

Write one sentence for each. At least two must be concrete and personal;
"the world ends" counts only if the story makes the reader feel one
person's loss inside it.

- **External:** what is lost in the world if they fail (the lighthouse
  goes dark and the ferry wrecks).
- **Relational:** who is lost, betrayed, or disappointed (her sister,
  whom she let go once before).
- **Internal:** what the protagonist becomes if they fail (someone who
  kept the light and let the people drown). This level connects to the
  character's lie; see `../../theme-craft/references/lie-truth.md`.

Stakes should rise: the draft will need a way to make each level worse by
the midpoint. If none can get worse, the premise will stall in the middle.

## Premise and counter-premise

The `premise` field in `story.md` is the controlling idea, value + cause:
*Duty fails because she keeps the light for people who are already gone.*
The `counter-premise` is the antagonist's or world's opposing argument,
in the same form. See `../../theme-craft/references/controlling-idea.md`.

Test: could a reasonable person believe the counter-premise? If not, the
antagonist is a straw man and the ending proves nothing.

## Common premise failures

- **Situation, not story.** A world or condition with no one pursuing
  anything (*a city where no one can lie*). Fix: add a person whose goal
  the condition blocks.
- **Twist, not story.** Everything depends on a reveal. Fix: ask what the
  protagonist wants before the reveal, and make that the story.
- **Passive sufferer.** Things happen to the protagonist. Fix: find the
  point where they choose, and move it earlier.
- **Two books.** Two strong premises fighting for the lead. Fix: choose
  one as the spine; the other becomes a subplot or the next book.
- **Small stakes told large.** A cosmic threat nobody on the page feels.
  Fix: shrink the scale until one relationship carries it.
