# Rhyme

## Kinds of rhyme

| Kind | What matches | Example |
|------|--------------|---------|
| Perfect | The stressed vowel and everything after it | *rock / clock*, *keeper / deeper* |
| Slant (near) | Only the vowel, or only the final consonants | *rock / talk*, *light / late* |
| Eye | Spelling only, not sound | *love / move*, *though / through* |
| Feminine | Two or more syllables, stress on the first | *tiding / hiding* |
| Identical | The same sound and word (or homophone) | *light / light*, *sea / see* |
| Internal | A rhyme inside the line, not at the end | "the *night* was *bright*" |

Perfect rhyme is the default for fixed forms and comic verse. Slant rhyme
is normal in song lyrics and much modern verse. Identical rhyme usually
reads as a failure, except in a refrain. Eye rhyme works only in poems
older than the pronunciation change, or as a deliberate joke.

Rhyme depends on accent. *Rock / clock* holds everywhere; *path / bath /
laugh* do not rhyme with *math* in most British accents, and *dawn /
don* rhyme in much of North America but not in Britain. Check rhymes
in the dialect the verse is for.

## Scheme notation

Give each new end sound the next letter; repeat the letter when a sound
returns. Mark refrains with a number (A1, A2) and slant rhymes with an
apostrophe (B').

```text
A keeper who lived on a rock      A
Whose lamp was as true as a clock.   A
When his clock lost a hand        B
He relied on the sand,            B
And he keeps his accounts in a sock.    A
```

rules in `forms.md`.

## Forced-rhyme tells

A rhyme is forced when the line exists for the rhyme word, not the other
way round. Check each rhyme pair for:

- **Inversion:** word order bent to put the rhyme last ("the storm did
  rage, the waves were high; / to shore the boat could not come nigh").
- **Filler verb:** *did* + verb to fit a beat or reach a rhyme.
- **Dead word:** a rhyme word that adds nothing, or a word the poem would
  never use elsewhere (*nigh*, *yore*, *anon* in modern verse).
- **Meaning drift:** the line changes subject to reach the rhyme.
- **Predictable pair:** the reader knows the rhyme before it arrives. The
  pairs below have been used so often that they read as defaults:
  *heart / apart*, *love / above*, *fire / desire*, *pain / again*,
  *sky / high / fly*, *night / light / bright*, *day / way / away*,
  *soul / whole*, *tears / years*, *sea / free*.

Fix a forced rhyme by changing the rhyme word in the *other* line of the
pair, or by rebuilding the sentence so the rhyme falls naturally. A
strong fresh rhyme usually comes from a concrete noun or verb.

## Checking a scheme

1. Write the last stressed vowel and following sounds of each line, not
   the spelling.
2. Assign letters.
3. Compare with the form. Note every slant or eye rhyme and ask whether
   the author meant it.
4. Check that no rhyme word repeats within a stanza unless it is a
   refrain.

## Rhyme in other languages

The kinds above are English. Other traditions define rhyme differently;
check the verse's `language` and follow its tradition:

- **French** classical verse alternates masculine rhymes and feminine
  rhymes (ending in a mute *e*), and values a *rime riche* that matches
  more sounds than the vowel.
- **Spanish** distinguishes consonant rhyme (*rima consonante*: every
  sound from the last stressed vowel) from assonant rhyme (*rima
  asonante*: only the vowels). The *romance* rhymes the even lines in
  assonance.
- **Classical Chinese** regulated verse rhymes on the even lines.
- **Japanese** haiku and tanka do not use rhyme.

In any language, a rhyme that holds in the spelling but not the
pronunciation is an eye rhyme, and dialect still decides what rhymes.
