# Verse Forms

Each form lists its rules, a short original example, and the faults to
check for. State the rules you are following before drafting; when the
user bends a form on purpose, follow the user.

## Limerick

- Five lines, rhyme scheme AABBA.
- Lines 1, 2, and 5 have three beats; lines 3 and 4 have two.
- Anapestic (da-da-DUM), usually starting with an unstressed syllable or
  two: "there WAS an old KEEP-er of GREY".
- Line 1 often names a person and a place, but that is convention, not a
  rule.
- Line 5 turns the joke. It may echo line 1's rhyme word, but a new word
  lands harder.

```text
A keeper who lived on a rock
Whose lamp was as true as a clock.
    When his clock lost a hand
    He relied on the sand,
And he keeps his accounts in a sock.
```

Faults: a line 5 with no turn; a limp beat count in lines 3 and 4; the
same rhyme word used twice in the A lines; stress forced onto a weak
syllable to keep the gallop ("the KEEP-er ex-PLAINED it to ME").

## Sonnet

Fourteen lines of iambic pentameter (five da-DUM feet).

- **Shakespearean:** ABAB CDCD EFEF GG. Three quatrains develop the idea;
  the closing couplet turns or sums it up.
- **Petrarchan:** an octave ABBAABBA and a sestet (CDECDE, CDCDCD, or a
  close variant). The turn (volta) comes at line 9.

Faults: no turn; a couplet that restates the quatrains; lines padded to
ten syllables with filler; inverted syntax to reach the rhyme.

## Haiku

- In English, the usual pattern is three lines of 5, 7, and 5 syllables.
  Many poets write shorter lines; follow the user. Japanese haiku count
  5, 7, and 5 *on* (morae), not syllables; see `meter-and-scansion.md`.
- One concrete image, often a season word (*kigo*) and a cut (*kireji*),
  a pause that sets two images side by side. In English a dash or line
  break does the cutting.
- Present tense, no rhyme, no abstract statement of feeling.

```text
paraffin runs low
the keeper tilts the last can —
a gull's shadow falls
```

Faults: syllables counted wrong (count aloud: *fire* is one or two
syllables depending on accent, so say which you used); a moral in line 3;
two ideas instead of one image.

## Villanelle

- Nineteen lines: five tercets and a closing quatrain.
- Two refrains: line 1 (A1) and line 3 (A2). A1 ends tercets 2 and 4; A2
  ends tercets 3 and 5; the quatrain ends with A1 then A2.
- Rhyme scheme ABA ABA ABA ABA ABA ABAA, on only two rhyme sounds.
- Usually iambic pentameter.

Faults: refrains that do not carry more meaning each time they return;
running out of B rhymes and forcing the last ones. Pick a B sound with
many common rhymes before drafting.

## Ballad

- Quatrains, usually rhyming ABCB (sometimes ABAB).
- Common meter: lines alternating four beats and three beats (8 and 6
  syllables in strict common meter).
- Tells a story, often with dialogue, a refrain, and incremental
  repetition (a line repeated with one word changed as the story moves).

Faults: summarising events instead of showing them; losing the beat in
dialogue lines; a refrain that does not fit every stanza it follows.

## Clerihew

- Four lines, AABB, irregular line lengths.
- Line 1 is, or ends with, a person's name. The rest is a comic,
  unlikely claim about them.

Faults: lines so regular it reads as a limerick fragment; a real living
person treated unkindly (check with the user).

## Couplets

- Rhymed pairs. Heroic couplets are iambic pentameter; shorter couplets
  (tetrameter) move faster and suit comic or children's verse.
- Each couplet often closes its sense on the rhyme.

Faults: every sense ending on the rhyme until it thuds; vary with
enjambment (a sentence that runs over the line end).

## Free verse

- No fixed meter or rhyme. Line breaks still carry meaning: they set pace
  and put weight on the last word of each line.
- Scan it anyway: note where the stresses cluster and whether line ends
  land on strong words.

Faults: prose chopped into lines at random; breaks on *the*, *of*, *and*
with no reason.

## Song lyrics

- Written to be sung: stresses must fall on the melody's strong beats,
  and long vowels on long notes.
- Verse, chorus, and often a bridge. The chorus repeats word for word
  (or near it) and carries the title or hook.
- Slant rhyme is normal in lyrics; the ear forgives more in song.
- A character's song in a story is read on the page, not heard: it needs
  to work as verse too.

Faults: lines with more syllables than the tune has notes; a chorus that
changes each time; quoting a real song's lyrics (never do this; see Hard
Rules).

## Rhyming picture-book text

- Plan the spreads first with the `adaptation` skill; one stanza or
  couplet per spread is common.
- The adult reading aloud has never seen the text: every line must scan
  on a cold read, with stress falling where normal speech puts it.
- Anapestic tetrameter (four da-da-DUM beats) and iambic tetrameter are
  common. Keep one meter through the book.
- Rhyme only if every line scans perfectly. Many publishers prefer prose
  picture books to rhyme that stumbles.

Faults: a word chosen for rhyme that a child would not know and the story
does not need; meter that only works in the author's accent; a refrain
that changes.
