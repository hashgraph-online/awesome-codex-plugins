### 2. Music prompt test (eleven_music_v2_5, 20 s asked, instrumental)

Detailed prompt written from the ElevenLabs Music best-practices page (answer genre, mood, instruments, tempo, era;
narrate the arrangement with "start with just ... then bring in ..."; mark the silence):
"Minimal electronic launch score, 120 BPM, in D minor, ... Start with just a soft muted synth pulse and a ticking
closed hi-hat for four bars. Then bring in a warm sub bass and a rising filtered pad for two bars, building. Then half
a bar of complete silence. Then one big low impact ... End on a single held chord that rings out and fades."
3 takes ($0.05 each) + 1 vague take ("upbeat electronic track for a product launch").

| take | length | measured BPM | bass after four bars | half-bar silence + impact + kick section |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| detailed 1 | 29.0 s | 120.0 | yes (~8 s) | no (pulse, then a 13 s ring-out) |
| detailed 2 | 20.0 s | 120.05 | yes (~8 s) | no |
| detailed 3 | 20.0 s | 119.97 | no (full from the start) | no |
| vague | 20.0 s | 128.0 | — | — |

- Stated BPM held in 3 of 3 (within 0.05 BPM); the arrangement's entry order in 2 of 3; the planned stop and hit in
  0 of 3; the asked length in 2 of 3.
- librosa `beat_track` reported 117.45 BPM for the 120 BPM takes (its tempo bins); the average beat gap gives 120.0.

- Conclusion used in the guide: ask the model for tempo, key, instruments and order; make stops, drops and the exact
  length in the edit (cut on a downbeat, room tone through the gap, impact on the next downbeat).

bed re-enters at 13.0, music ducked 8 dB under lines (voice band 100%, lows 35%, highs 65%; 4 frames down, 24 up),
cuts on bar lines. Plain = vague bed at full level, no ducking,

## Voice (107)

- Replacement dialogue usually sounds lifeless because it is recorded as a technical chore in a dead booth; record or place it in a real acoustic space. — "Why not record ADR on location, in real-world places which will inspire the actors and provide realistic acoustics?" — Randy Thom (Director of sound design at Skywalker Sound (Wikipedia); sound designer and re-recording mixer on Forrest Gump, Contact, Cast Away (filmsound.org Randy Thom …). http://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm
- Wall-to-wall dialogue, plus grunts and breaths stuffed into every gap, leaves no room for sound; cut lines. — "Eventually, all the space not occupied by actual words is filled with grunts, groans, and breathing (supposedly in an effort to "keep the character alive")." — Randy Thom (Director of sound design at Skywalker Sound (Wikipedia); sound designer and re-recording mixer on Forrest Gump, Contact, Cast Away (filmsound.org Randy Thom …). http://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm
- Pure synthesizer voices sounded cold and soulless; R2-D2 worked as half human performance, half electronics. — "I went to synthesizers at first but the results sounded too much like a machine, just something cold, didn't seem to have a soul." — Ben Burtt (Sound designer of the Star Wars and Indiana Jones series, E.T., WALL-E (Wikipedia; NPR)). https://www.npr.org/transcripts/131968222
- Slipping a voice out of sync with the lips (the voice arrives after the mouth moves) made Dune's 'Voice' feel supernatural. — "He slipped the sync so that Paul uttered the words but his voice came seconds after we see him speak on screen." — Mark Mangini describing editor Joe Walker's idea (Dune) (Oscar for Best Sound, Dune (HPA)). https://hpaonline.com/composing-with-sound-a-conversation-with-hpa-award-winners-mark-mangini-and-ron-bartlett/
- Pick the voice that makes you feel the character's emotion, not the one you admire intellectually; impressions and accents are not acting. — "Target people who, in their reads, make you feel something personal and emotionally resonant." — Khris Brown (Gamasutra Q&A) (Voice director 20+ years; helped launch the LucasArts Voice Department (1992); creative director, Ubisoft Montreal Narrative Talent Group (Gamasutra)). https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/q-a-veteran-director-khris-brown-on-the-secrets-of-great-voice-acting
- If the voice cannot be done well, ship without it; bad voice destroys the experience. — "Better no voice at all than poor voice." — Khris Brown (Voice director; LucasArts Voice Department). https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/q-a-veteran-director-khris-brown-on-the-secrets-of-great-voice-acting
- Direct a voice by naming the role, the listener and precise adjectives, not by asking for a tone in the abstract. — "Make everything into a role, because that's what it is -- who is speaking? Neutral bystander? Impassioned preacher? Wry humorist? Helpful teacher? Caring nurse? Smiling enthusiast?" — Diane Havens, quoted by voice actor Vicki Amorose (Gamasutra) (Professional voice-over actors (Amorose with Havens, JS Gilbert, Bill Painter, Kevin Cooke)). https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/directing-the-voice-over-actor-tips-for-better-communication
- 'Conversational' is too vague; give a concrete situation instead. — "Instead of saying, "Make it more conversational", it would be helpful to say something like, "Toss off that last phrase like it's something you've discussed a million times", or "I don't feel like you are speaking directly to me."" — Vicki Amorose (Voice-over actor (Gamasutra)). https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/directing-the-voice-over-actor-tips-for-better-communication
- Actors recorded together, reacting to each other, sound more real than lines recorded in isolation. — "This approach to direction can yield a much more realistic performance because the actors are no longer working in a vacuum, but rather reacting to the performance and delivery of another human being." — Jesse Harlin ('Thinking Outside the Booth', G.A.N.G. column) (Game audio professional; Game Audio Network Guild column). https://www.audiogang.org/?p=2588
- Give the voice performer the picture (animation, storyboards) and a description of each physical action, so exertions and deliveries match. — "Animation or even storyboards of what will show on screen can help an actor tailor their delivery." — Bonnie Bogovich (Game Developer) (Voice Director, Unlock Audio). https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/how-to-get-the-most-out-of-your-voice-actors
- A full breath at the start of a thought gives the line energy. — "When you start a thought with a full, supported breath, your speech has more energy, so you can better communicate your story." — Jessica Hansen (NPR Training) (NPR announcer and in-house voice coach). https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/28/g-s1-65460/aerobics-for-your-voice-3-tips-for-sounding-better-on-air
- After writing narration, the first job is to cut it; pictures beat words. — "Having written a commentary, the first thing to do is to carefully see what you can get rid of." — Sir David Attenborough (BBC naturalist and narrator for eight decades (Life on Earth, Blue Planet, Planet Earth III)). https://www.qradio.com/archive/a-picture-is-more-powerful-than-a-spoken-word-sir-david-attenborough-doesnt-want-to-talk-too-much-on-his-wildlife-shows
- Ken Burns' team screens a 'radio cut' (interviews, narration and voices only) before cutting picture. — "Yes, it's just a radio cut. We screen with just the talking heads and the narration and any voices that there might be." — Craig Mellish, ACE (Art of the Cut) (Editor at Ken Burns' Florentine Films ~20 years (National Parks, Vietnam, Country Music); two Primetime Emmy nominations for sound editing). https://www.provideocoalition.com/art-cut-ken-burns-doc-editor-craig-mellish-ace/

## Film craft (30)

- Keep no more than five simultaneous sound layers in any five-second stretch, and spread them across dialogue, music and different kinds of effects. — "What I am suggesting is that, at any one moment (for practical purposes, let's say that a 'moment' is any five-second section of film), five layers is the maximum that can be tolerated by an audience if you also want them to maintain a clear sense of the individual elements that are contributing to the mix." — Walter Murch (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://web.archive.org/web/20260905170354/https://transom.org/2005/walter-murch/
- Sounds of the same kind (two voices, two pieces of music, many footsteps) blur after about two and a half layers, so clear everything else when two people talk. — "If the sounds stack up in one region (one color), the limit shrinks to two-and-a-half. If you want to have two-and-a-half layers of dialogue, for instance, and you want people to understand every word, you had better eliminate the competition from any other sounds which might be running at the same time." — Walter Murch (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://web.archive.org/web/20260905170354/https://transom.org/2005/walter-murch/
- Sync every footstep for one or two characters, but for three or more the audience hears a group, so pace and surface matter more than sync. — "if there was one robot, his footsteps had to be in sync; if there were two robots, also, their footsteps had to be in sync; but if there were three robots, nothing had to be in sync." — Walter Murch (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://web.archive.org/web/20260905170354/https://transom.org/2005/walter-murch/
- To make room for a key line, pull one layer (often the music) out for that moment and bring it back right after; the audience does not notice it leave. — "Under the circumstances, music was the sacrificial victim. The miraculous thing is that you do not hear it go away – you believe that it is still playing" — Walter Murch (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://web.archive.org/web/20260905170354/https://transom.org/2005/walter-murch/
- Keep dialogue (and plain footsteps) in the centre and give music and room ambience the wide stereo field. — "As a general rule, then, the "warmer" the sound, the more it tends to be given a full stereo (multitrack) treatment, whereas the "cooler" the sound, the more it tends to be monophonically placed in the center." — Walter Murch (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://web.archive.org/web/20260905170354/https://transom.org/2005/walter-murch/
- Make a clean recording sound like it lives in a real place by playing it through a speaker in that kind of space and re-recording it, then blend dry and room versions. — "George [Lucas] and I took the master track of the two-hour radio show with Wolfman Jack as DJ and played it back on a Nagra in a real space - a suburban backyard." — Walter Murch (quoted from 'Sound Doctrine: An Interview with Walter Murch') (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://filmsound.org/terminology/worldizing.htm
- Background music under dialogue works better when it is made diffuse and out of focus (distance, room), not just turned down or filtered. — "We can still have the music in the background, but because it's so diffuse, you can't find edges to focus on and, therefore, the dialogue which is in the foreground and which is in focus is clearly what you're supposed to be listening to." — Walter Murch (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://filmsound.org/terminology/worldizing.htm
- Remove the obvious sounds and add one that seems slightly at odds with the picture, so the audience fills the gap with feeling. — "by choosing carefully what to eliminate, and then adding back sounds that seem at first hearing to be somewhat at odds with the accompanying image, the filmmaker can open up a perceptual vacuum into which the mind of the audience must inevitably rush." — Walter Murch (NYT essay 'Stretching Sound to Help the Mind See') (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://filmsound.org/murch/stretching.htm
- At the biggest emotional moment you can take the expected sound away (a silent scream) and release it later. — "Instead of a calm face with a scream, we see a screaming face in silence." — Walter Murch (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://filmsound.org/murch/stretching.htm
- Use full silence only a few times per film so it stays surprising, and get into it gradually so it does not sound like a fault. — "But I try to use silence in unexpected ways three or four times in every film. I cant use it more often or it would become predictable." — Walter Murch (FTII interview, Pune) (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://filmsound.org/murch/interview-with-walter-murch.htm
- At a scene change, hold the outgoing shot about 30% longer than an ordinary cut, and the first new shot slightly longer too. — "In general, at the moment of a transition, the last shot of the outgoing scene is held 30 % longer than it would if it were a cut within the body of the scene." — Walter Murch (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://filmsound.org/murch/interview-with-walter-murch.htm
- Bring the next scene's sound in before its picture (pre-lap); it makes the audience listen harder. — "if you use pre-laps, it sensitises the audience to sound." — Walter Murch (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). http://filmsound.org/murch/interview-with-walter-murch.htm
- A sudden drop of the background sound to silence tells the audience that something bad is about to happen. — "So we used a sudden transition to silence as an indication of danger. Something big is about to happen." — Walter Murch (Web of Stories interview, The English Patient) (Film editor and sound designer: Apocalypse Now, The Godfather I-III, The Conversation, The English Patient; 3 Oscars incl. the double for editing + sound on …). https://www.webofstories.com/play/56606
- The strongest sound sequences are heard through one character's point of view, so decide whose ears we are in. — "Most of the great sound sequences in films are "pov" sequences." — Randy Thom ('Designing a Movie for Sound') (Director of sound design at Skywalker Sound (Wikipedia); sound designer and re-recording mixer on Forrest Gump, Contact, Cast Away (filmsound.org Randy Thom …). http://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm
- Leave moments after or before dialogue where the character just listens, so the place can speak. — "The editor and director need to be able to figure out when it will be useful to linger on a shot after the dialog is finished, or before it begins. To stay around after the obvious "action" is past, so that we can listen." — Randy Thom (Director of sound design at Skywalker Sound (Wikipedia); sound designer and re-recording mixer on Forrest Gump, Contact, Cast Away (filmsound.org Randy Thom …). http://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm
- Show the source of an important background sound once in a close shot; after that it can play off-screen and the audience will know what it is. — "If there is at least a brief, fairly close shot of some machine which could be making the sound, it will help me immensely to establish the sound. Over that shot we can feature the sound, placing it firmly in the minds of the audience. Then we never have to see it again" — Randy Thom (Director of sound design at Skywalker Sound (Wikipedia); sound designer and re-recording mixer on Forrest Gump, Contact, Cast Away (filmsound.org Randy Thom …). http://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm
- Build otherworldly sounds from real recordings (motors, animals, appliances) changed until they are hard to recognise, rather than from synthesizers. — "don't go to your synthesizers first and generate echoing electronic tones for me, but rather go out into the real world with your tape recorder and record motors and animals and bring back familiar sounds, which we can modify into something otherworldly." — J.W. Rinzler describing George Lucas's brief to Ben Burtt (NPR Weekend Edition) (Rinzler: author of 'The Sounds of Star Wars'; Burtt: sound designer of the Star Wars and Indiana Jones series, E.T., WALL-E (Wikipedia)). https://www.npr.org/transcripts/131968222
- Even in a battle, each effect must be readable: separate them in frequency, time and space instead of piling them up loud. — "Even within the chaos of a war movie, I believe that articulating the sound effects is vital; too often loudness and density in a track obscure any concept of what is going on. We paid attention to the relative frequencies of effects, and their rhythmic, sequential placement" — Gary Rydstrom (excerpt from Holman, 'Surround Sound', 2nd ed.) (Sound designer/re-recording mixer, 7 Academy Awards: Terminator 2, Jurassic Park, Titanic, Saving Private Ryan (Designing Sound bio)). https://usoproject.blogspot.com/2009/11/saving-private-ryan-music-sound.html
- Before a shock, pull the backgrounds and footsteps down so the audience leans in; the shock lands harder. — "When Chigurh is approaching Moss's motel room in Del Rio we underplayed everything: BG's, footsteps, etc., to help create tension." — Craig Berkey (No Country for Old Men) (Sound designer and re-recording mixer, No Country for Old Men (Oscar-nominated sound mix; CAS award per Mix)). https://designingsound.org/2007/11/no-country-for-old-men-exclusive-interview-with-sound-designerre-r
- Winds and backgrounds can do the job of a score if each area has its own and they change within the scene. — "To me the winds were the score throughout this part of the film." — Craig Berkey (Sound designer/re-recording mixer, No Country for Old Men). https://designingsound.org/2007/11/no-country-for-old-men-exclusive-interview-with-sound-designerre-r
- A string of tiny off-screen sounds (a phone ringing unanswered, a chair scrape, floor creaks) makes the audience focus hard before the threat appears. — "There are several things that happen off screen early in the sequence that force you to focus on the smallest of sounds." — Craig Berkey (Sound designer/re-recording mixer, No Country for Old Men). https://designingsound.org/2007/11/no-country-for-old-men-exclusive-interview-with-sound-designerre-r
- Loud hits are more startling when a little silence comes right before them. — "Loud sounds like explosions are more startling and effective if they're preceded by a little silence." — Richard King (Reddit AMA, via IndieWire) (Supervising sound editor; 4 Oscars: Dunkirk, Inception, The Dark Knight, Master and Commander). https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/christopher-nolan-sound-designer-spills-190237647.html
- To move between worlds or times, slow and re-pitch the real-world sounds so they turn into the next scene's sound. — "So all the sounds slow and change and become a different sound in the next sequence – interior jet roar becomes traffic when we transition to the first dream level, for instance." — Richard King (Inception) (4 Oscars incl. Inception). https://designingsound.org/2010/08/18/inception-exclusive-interview-with-richard-king/
- Hold detailed point-by-point spotting sessions where the director says what each moment is about; they become the sound bible and stop clutter. — "With a film with this much density and action, it's easy to clutter up a sound track. These spotting sessions became a bible and point of inspiration." — Mark Mangini (Mad Max: Fury Road) (Supervising sound editor/sound designer; Oscars for Mad Max: Fury Road and Dune (HPA)). https://www.btlnews.com/awards/contenders-sound-editors-mark-mangini-and-scott-hecker-mad-max-fury-road/
- Sweeten machines with animal sounds that carry the story's metaphor. — "When you'd see a harpoon skewer the War Rig's tanker, we'd sweeten it with hump back whales moaning and growling." — Mark Mangini (Oscars for Fury Road and Dune). https://www.btlnews.com/awards/contenders-sound-editors-mark-mangini-and-scott-hecker-mad-max-fury-road/
- Six weeks of sound redesign alone raised Fury Road's preview score by eight points. — "We tested the film a second time and the scores went up eight points – and that's a huge jump" — Mark Mangini (Oscars for Fury Road and Dune). https://www.btlnews.com/awards/contenders-sound-editors-mark-mangini-and-scott-hecker-mad-max-fury-road/
- Place every off-screen sound on a map at a real distance; with distance you lose bass and top end and gain reflections. — "making a map, a big map, that was based on the photograph from Google Images, but all with specific distances and understanding how sound decays over time and how the tonality so that you lose the bass and the top end of a sound incident, and all the reflective surfaces." — Johnnie Burn (The Zone of Interest) (Won the 2024 Academy Award for Best Sound for The Zone of Interest; founder of Wave Studios (Wikipedia)). https://nofilmschool.com/the-zone-of-interest-sound
- For each sound, look at the surfaces in the image and add a quieter duplicate and an echo where a wall would reflect it, because ears are hard to fool. — "For every sound, I'd look at the reflective surfaces in the image, and I'd make sure I had a duplicate sound if it was quieter and an echoey bounce if there was a wall there." — Johnnie Burn (2024 Oscar, Best Sound). https://www.motionpictures.org/2024/01/the-zone-of-interest-sound-designer-johnnie-burn-on-creating-the-soundscape-from-hell/
- When a character reacts to a sound, place the sound about half a second before the reaction. — "Normally, I'm making background soundscape, and you'd be looking for an actor's reaction, say a dog bark, and put it a half second before that" — Johnnie Burn (2024 Oscar, Best Sound). https://www.motionpictures.org/2024/01/the-zone-of-interest-sound-designer-johnnie-burn-on-creating-the-soundscape-from-hell/
- Sound makes viewers believe they see things that the sound itself supplies, so good sound makes plain or ambiguous images read as specific. — "By added value I mean the expressive and informative value with which a sound enriches a given image so as to create the definite impression, in the immediate or remembered experience one has of it, that this information or expression "naturally" comes from what is seen and is already contained in the image itself." — Michel Chion ('Audio-Vision', ch. 1) (French film theorist and composer; author of Audio-Vision (Wikipedia)). https://dalena.github.io/art5001/supplements/MICHEL_CHION_PROJECTIONS_OF_SOUND_ON_IMAGE.pdf

## Trailers, ads, branding (15)

- Build a trailer's rhythm from diegetic sounds of the film (a head hitting a chalkboard, a cough, a crash) remixed into a beat. — "that sound of this man's skull throbbing against a chalkboard really became kind of the baseline for the trailer and kind of set the trailer's rhythm." — Mark Woollen (NPR Fresh Air) (Trailer maker for Schindler's List, The Social Network, Boyhood, Birdman, The Theory of Everything, A Serious Man). https://www.npr.org/transcripts/388481464
- Introduce a sound effect without its picture, then repeat it in the next bar with the picture, so it turns sinister. — "A trick I like to use is to introduce a sound effect without the picture attached to it. And then in the next measure of music I will have the sound effect again but now with the picture." — Tommy Malatesta (via Jonny Elwyn's summary of James Rowney's 'Making The Cut') (Creative Director/Editor at AV Squad; A24 trailers (Hereditary, The Northman, Everything Everywhere All At Once)). https://jonnyelwyn.co.uk/film-and-video-editing/inside-professional-trailer-editing/
- Build the drum track from sound effects (clicks, punches, noises) and music stems rather than a stock drum loop. — "I'll use stems of tracks as sound effects. I never want drums. You can always use sound effects to create those drum tracks (clicks, punches, noises)" — Tommy Malatesta (AV Squad, A24 trailers). https://jonnyelwyn.co.uk/film-and-video-editing/inside-professional-trailer-editing/
- Change the music's section after each piece of information lands; SFX and action beats are punctuation in between. — "It's about making sure that you always have progression in your music, and each one of those progressions should happen after you've finished giving a piece of information. They're chapter breaks." — Adam Gonzales (via Jonny Elwyn / James Rowney) (Editor and Creative Director, AV Squad). https://jonnyelwyn.co.uk/film-and-video-editing/inside-professional-trailer-editing/
- Trailer houses build custom 'braaams' from the film's own sounds (a dinosaur roar for Jurassic World) rather than copying Inception. — "trailer companies will craft signature braaams, such as the one made out of a dinosaur roar customized for the Jurassic World teaser." — The Hollywood Reporter, with Bobby Gumm (head of music, Trailer Park) (Trailer Park cut Mad Max: Fury Road and Mission: Impossible - Rogue Nation trailers). https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/braaams-beginners-how-a-horn-793220
- In ads, dynamics get squashed and music and dialogue fill everything, so choose a few moments for sound to shine and get out of the way elsewhere. — "So you really have to pick your moments to shine and pick your moments to get out of the way." — Kip Smedley (A Sound Effect) (Sound effects editor; 16 years in advertising (Machine Head) before features and TV). https://www.asoundeffect.com/sound-for-commercials-kip-smedley/
- Finish the longest hero cut first, then cut 60/30-second versions from its best parts, and a 7-second version that is just the punch line. — "The 2 minute and 13 second one is usually the one that we finish first. Then the 30 or 60 second spot has all the best parts from that, including the best sound design." — Tom Disher (A Sound Effect) (Composer/sound designer, owner of Disher Sound, 28+ years in film). https://www.asoundeffect.com/sound-for-ads/
- Grade the sound like the picture: an old-film section mixed in mono and degraded with worn tape. — "Hence, the 1930's horror section was mixed in mono and then degraded using an old, worn out tape effect." — Factory Studios sound designers (Dan Beckwith, Mark Hills, Phil Bolland) (London ad sound studio: Honda 'Hands', John Lewis Christmas ads; Cannes Lions, BAFTA, D&AD awards). https://asoundeffect.com/sound-for-commercials
- A sonic logo for streaming must be very short, because people want to click and play. — "First off and arguably most important, it had to be really short." — Todd Yellin (Netflix VP of Product, on Twenty Thousand Hertz) (Led the creation of the Netflix 'ta-dum'). https://www.20k.org/episodes/netflix
- A designed sound is usually several sounds sweetened together; the ta-dum is a ring knocking on wood plus anvil, muted hits and a reversed guitar 'blossom'. — "Any sound is made up of four sounds, generally." — Lon Bender (Twenty Thousand Hertz) (Supervising sound editor; Oscar for Braveheart sound effects editing; nominations for The Revenant, Drive (Wikipedia)). https://www.20k.org/episodes/netflix
- Netflix tested candidates by playing one sound to people who didn't know its purpose and collecting the words it evoked; 'movie' won. — "And then see what words come to their mind and what they associate. And then, thousands of other people try a different sound." — Todd Yellin (Netflix VP of Product). https://www.20k.org/episodes/netflix
- The Intel jingle starts with a throwaway note that clears whatever sound came before, so the melody that follows is heard and remembered. — "There is this initial energy burst which closes off whatever sound was there before. It prepares you for the melody, then the melody comes in." — Walter Werzowa (Globe and Mail) (Composer of the Intel bong; founder of Musikvergnuegen (Wikipedia)). https://www.theglobeandmail.com/report-on-business/the-mozart-of-jingles/article4211353
- The Intel motif's rhythm came from the syllables of 'Intel Inside', and its sound (timbre) proved more recognisable than the melody itself. — "Among a 60-person focus group, researchers found only 80 percent of participants recognized the correct melody played on a violin, but 100 percent recognized it with the proper sound -- even when an incorrect note was added." — Silicon Valley Watcher reporting Walter Werzowa / Intel (Werzowa composed the Intel bong (1991-94); Dennis Carter was Intel's marketing chief). https://www.siliconvalleywatcher.com/20-year-anniversary-of-intel-bong----innovative-marketing-drove-billions-in-chip-sales/
- Draft the content, note the emotions felt and wanted, then build several different playlists and audition styles before locking the track. — "When I kick off a project, I usually draft the content first, note the emotions I felt during and the emotions I want to achieve, and then put together multiple varied playlists based on those feelings." — Hayden Hillier-Smith (Musicbed) (Editor for MrBeast, Logan Paul, Mark Rober; 2020 Streamy Award for best editing; co-host of The Editing Podcast). https://www.musicbed.com/articles/?p=7556
- Use music and stylised editing for most of the piece so that removing them at the serious moment hits hard. — "They let us settle into the language of the film, so they can take it away from us. So when it came to the deeper, more serious moments, they purposefully held back the editing. There was no music, slow cuts, no jump cuts, or continuity issues." — Hayden Hillier-Smith describing Civil War (editor Jake Roberts) (Editor for MrBeast, Logan Paul; 2020 Streamy Award). https://www.musicbed.com/articles/?p=7556
