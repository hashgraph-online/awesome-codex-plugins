# Taste: how a judgement call is made

A reference decides what a move does, never how it lands in this product. The decision is rebuilt in the product's own components, spacing and words, and some parts no screen shows at all: where a new label sits, what it says, where a displaced control lands. Those calls are your judgement. Left alone, judgement drifts to the same habits in every product: a gradient, a pill badge, an icon beside every label, a subtitle restating the title, a card around everything. This file replaces those habits with rules that read the product in front of you. Steps 4 to 6 of the skill use it.

## Where it applies

Every move, in how it is built. Authority runs in this order:

1. The Preserve list and the design system.
2. This file, for how every part is built: its size, rank, spacing, alignment and wording.
3. The cited screens, for what each move does: the decision it makes and where its parts sit.

A cited screen informs a move but never decides how a part is built. When a screen and this file disagree, this file wins: a screen that makes a part big doesn't make it big here if this product's job ranks it lower. This file never starts a move; step 4 decides what gets a move. A part a cited screen shows is covered by its References line. A part no cited screen shows is your judgement, and the report grades it so, as Ground rules set out. These rules are how the call was made, not evidence, and are never named to the user.

## Dealbreakers

A render that breaks one is fixed before capture, whatever a reference shows.

1. **Don't add for the sake of adding.** Every element the render gains answers a move's problem. Empty space is not a problem: leave it, and add no icon, badge, divider, shadow, background panel or illustration a move does not need. Step 4's cut for stock details still applies.
2. **Don't add text the job does not need.** No subtitle restating a heading, no helper line under a clear control, no label on something the screen already explains. When a move needs words, write the shortest plain label, in the case, tense and tone of the copy around it, claiming nothing the source or the user's words do not show.
3. **Don't add a gradient the design system does not have.** Use one only where a token or an existing component already does. Otherwise use the flat colour role of the nearest sibling.
4. **Don't blend sides.** A render, or a variation, carries only its own side's decisions, even where another side's screen looks better for one part. That part stays as the baseline has it, or as the taken side's screens show it.

## The rules

None says how anything looks; the look is the product's. They decide only relations.

1. **Change the least that answers the problem.** Rearrange before you restyle. A part the move does not need keeps its position, size and treatment. This limits what a move touches, never how fully the taken side is adopted.
2. **A new element copies its nearest sibling**: the component, size, weight, colour role and spacing of the closest element doing the same kind of job. Nothing gets a treatment the screen does not already use.
3. **One thing leads.** A new or changed part sits at or below the rank of what it serves and never outranks the first step of the job, whatever rank it has on a cited screen. Giving one thing more weight gives nothing else more.
4. **Things sit beside what they are about.** A label goes on the side of its subject the product already uses, usually above or leading. The gap inside a group is smaller than the gap between groups.
5. **Use the lines already there.** A new element aligns to an existing edge and sits on an existing spacing step, adding no new alignment line.
6. **The same thing looks the same.** Two elements doing one job in the target get one treatment.
7. **The platform's positions hold.** Primary action, back, close and dismiss go where the platform puts them, as the product already does.

## Reading the result

At step 6, check the dealbreakers first, then compare each added or changed part with its sibling. If a reader could tell which part was added by how it looks, rule 2 was broken. If the part draws the eye before the thing it serves, rule 3 was.
