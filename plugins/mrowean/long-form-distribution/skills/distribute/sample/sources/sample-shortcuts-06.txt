Three weeks in, I'm posting on a schedule instead of from memory. Since September 12 I've logged 47 posts, about 14 a week, against about 9 a week since May. More than anything, it's taken the remembering out of it.

Every issue I write has ten or more sections, and every section could be its own post.

That's the compounding: I put the effort into one long, careful piece, and the tool chops it into a steady run of short posts, which cuts the time I spend writing short-form from scratch.

It doesn't post for me. The point is to remove the remembering, not the judgment.

Since #01 I've ended working sessions with a /retro: whatever went wrong becomes a rule future sessions are supposed to follow, a memory of your own mistakes. I've run well over 120 retros this year, and they've grown to 394 rules.

I found out the hard way, most of my rules never reached the agent at all.

Two days later, Claude Code shipped its own version, /doctor prompt-audit. While it ran, it discovered my rules hook was printing 43,808 characters into every session causing only a fraction of it to be read.

The problem is accumulation. The more your setup learns, the longer its instructions get. Claude caps what a hook can load at 10,000 characters; past that, the model gets a 2,000-character preview and a file path, the rest sits unread.

Of 394 rules, about 15 reached a session at startup. Between July and late September, 41 of 111 retros, 37%, rediscovered a lesson already in the file. One lesson had been written down five separate times. 67% of the rules had never been cited once.

In the week since, 322 of the 394 rules have reached the model at least once, up from about 15. The script has surfaced a rule 1,534 times across 47 sessions. Lessons that hadn't been seen in months showed up the moment they were relevant. The cost: about two-thirds of a second per message, before Claude starts its answer. I haven't noticed it.

If you load anything into an agent at the start (a rules file, a project brief, a style guide), keep what a hook prints under 10,000 characters and put the rest somewhere the agent can search.

Eligibility. I run a scan of art open calls, and the tedious part is the fine print: age limits, region, medium, career stage.

In a test on 2,545 past listings, where Jev confidently rejected a listing my filter had passed, it was right 96% of the time (I checked 47 by hand). It now shadow-runs on every new listing, 1,210 listings so far, agreeing with my filter 78% of the time.

Taste. Picking which posts in my feed were worth my time. Replayed over 51 days of my feed, it essentially tied the keyword score I already use.

Why it failed: the information it needed can't be written. Taste lives in my head. Judgment needs context from elsewhere. A sorter can't go get it. Where it was confident, it was mostly on the easy calls I didn't need help with. Eligibility lives in the listing and runs at volume, so it worked.

Instinct is a personal assistant you text or call. Say "I need to be in New York tonight" and it books the flight, hotel and ride, updates your calendar and uses any travel credit you have. It also answers email, chases refunds and argues with customer support. It's invite-only, five invites per user (Fortune).

40% of users handed it a credit card within three weeks, Shinn said on Patrick O'Shaughnessy's podcast.

When agents hold your card, can access your accounts and know your preferences to decide where you buy, it opens a whole new arms race for customer acquisition.

If you sell, your next customer may be an agent reading your page: price, stock, shipping and returns it can understand, or it moves on. Making pages readable to AI agents and answer engines (often called AEO, answer engine optimization) is becoming a massive business.

The terms put the risk on you. What Instinct agrees to for you is "binding on you as if entered into directly by you," so a nonrefundable booking it makes counts as one you made.

Limits shrink the damage, but you still need to check the work.
